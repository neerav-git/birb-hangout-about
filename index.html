birb-hangout

A small social game about passing time together

Birb Hangout is a small multiplayer world I made for friends and family who live far away.

They nudged me to build something that could bridge that distance, and I began imagining a peaceful place with no overbearing goal: somewhere to read, play music, watch television or rest by the fire. Capturing the joy of simply remaining in one another’s company.

Additionally it is a place where birds can do what birds do: fly together!

Why I made it

Building a small hearth across distance

Reading Stephen Fry’s Mythos made me think about the hearth (an inviting fireplace)as a shared center, what the author mentions has gone missing from the current social fabric—a place where people could tell stories, sit quietly, and remain in one another’s company without needing a goal.

Why the idea of the hearth mattered

The idea mattered to me because there is a collective feeling of going back to the analog, when interactions were tangible , birb-hangout acknowledges that a virtual world cannot replace a real world interaction with your loved ones. The game is not made to keep you hooked by social goals or bogged by chat interfaces. Its made with the intent of capturing the feeling of connection and making it as the sole reward.

Birb Hangout is a place distant friends can inhabit together, with shared objects and activities giving each visit its own rhythm.

Activities

Ways to spend time together

Books, records, television, games, rest, gestures, and flight give people different ways to gather without demanding the same mood or pace from everyone. A kind of chaotic calm and a safe space to be yourself with your friends.

The story behind the activitiesThe design philosophy emerged from asking how another person could see and join you in what you were doing.

Design philosophy

Make the activities feel grounded in the world

The question

Reading books together, playing music, and watching television all happen in the game world without overlays or floating screens. This is diegetic interaction: the interface belongs to the world instead of replacing it. You can look up from a book and notice a friend across the room doing their own thing.

Why keeping the activity in the room changes the experience

A full-screen panel can make the shared world disappear at the moment someone begins using it.

Many modern games use overlays: reading a book or piece of paper opens a floating menu that hides the game screen. Birb Hangout instead renders an actual book in the room to recreate the feeling of reading a physical copy. The television follows the same principle. Overlays are limited to setup, so the world remains visible during media interactions and an accompanying friend can see someone browsing the vinyl cabinet, watching television, reading, or sleeping and decide whether to join without navigating a menu.

Methodology · Building activities into the world

Each activity begins as a sequence of visible actions on a shared object. The record player uses a timed handling sequence from sleeve selection to playback; reading combines a page renderer with a world-space book and seated pose; television combines an in-room screen, a physical remote, and a sofa camera. Beat tables define when an object changes hands, when media begins, and which pose other players should see.

Three.js renders the room, props, characters, cameras, and interaction states. React supplies focused setup controls where text entry or media selection is necessary, while Colyseus carries the shared result back into the room. This division keeps configuration concise and makes the activity itself legible through the bird, furniture, and object.

In the Lounge

The room begins with familiar things friends do together in a shared space, with opportunities for fantastical experiences such as dreaming together.

01 Music

Pick a record and change the mood of the room

Putting on a record and settling inSilent loop

Putting on a record should feel like choosing the music at a friend’s house. A bird browses the cabinet, carries a sleeve across the room, and places the record on the turntable while everyone nearby sees the choice unfold. The short ritual creates anticipation before the music begins and turns a private selection into a contribution to the room’s atmosphere.

How it was built

The cabinet turns choosing music into a visible sequence: browse a sleeve, carry it across the lounge, slide out the record, and lower it onto the platter.

Three.js keeps the cabinet, sleeve, record, and turntable inside the same room. The Colyseus server stores the shared collection and current selection, while playback begins when the record reaches the turntable so the media follows the bird’s action.

02 Books

Read without losing the person across from you

Opening a book and reading in the loungeSilent loop

Two rocking chairs face each other in the library so reading can be quiet without becoming solitary. The close view keeps the text readable, but a player can look up at any time and find the other bird still sitting across the room. Each person reads at a private pace while sharing the pauses, page turns, and comfortable silence of the same space.

A smaller activity

Build the shelf together

Players choose which books appear in the lounge library. The selected titles become visible spines on the shelf for everyone in the room, so another visitor can browse the collection by walking over rather than opening a separate catalogue.

How it was built

The local player needs crisp, controllable text; everyone else needs to see a bird holding and reading a believable book.

The Three.js scene therefore renders the activity twice. A close reader lays EPUB and PDF pages onto readable textures, while a separate world model keeps the book and reading pose visible to other players. The turning sheet bends across a mesh instead of rotating like a rigid card.

03 Television

Watch together without leaving the lounge

Choosing a view and watching from the sofaSilent loop

Watching begins with one bird putting something on and the others deciding whether to gather around. A physical remote shows who controls playback, while the sofa camera keeps the screen, birds, and lounge in the same view. Players can talk over what is playing or simply watch together without the video replacing the room. Its the surreal feeling of watching the real world from an animated game.

Choosing what to watch

Put something on for everyone

Everyone else can see who is controlling playback. Underneath that simple system, each browser converges on the same video, play state, and position to ensure everything is synced in real time.

Settling in

Settle in without losing the room

Once seated, players can talk, react, or watch without continually managing the camera, and the shared room remains part of the viewing experience. There is also a first person/ pov mode to view the screen with more immersion.

How it was built

Embedded video can load late or buffer differently in each browser.

The Colyseus room stores the selected video, whether it should be playing, and the shared playhead. One player temporarily owns the remote; other clients follow that state and correct drift instead of competing for control. The server synchronizes playback instructions rather than transmitting video frames, keeping the shared interaction lightweight.

04 Social play

Mini-games, trivia, and socializing with emotes

Mini-games such as trivia and emotes such as a hug, dance, or cheer add another layer of social interaction.

Trivia

Make an answer feel like an event

Players choose answers together, then the television begins a shared countdown. A wrong answer launches the losing bird and its seat while everyone watches the same reveal and can react to the same joke.

How it was built

The reveal, score, camera move, and physical gag run as one event on every screen.

The server sends one ceremony start time. Each browser derives the current beat from elapsed time and renders that moment locally, so a late frame does not permanently shift one player’s version of the joke.

A shared gesture

Meet another bird with a hug

The hug brings two birds together for a short shared animation. Quick emotes use a separate hold-and-release dial for smaller reactions such as a nod or cheer.

How gestures were built

Shared gestures coordinate the same approach, contact, and release on both screens while keeping the characters aligned.

Quick reactions use a radial selector: hold, point toward a choice, and release. Longer two-bird interactions coordinate the participants as one timed sequence.

Methodology · Character rigging and shared gestures

The bird is assembled as a procedural Three.js character with named body, face, wing, tail, and contact points. A shared pose vocabulary drives standing, walking, carrying, reading, sleeping, emotes, and flight, giving each activity access to the same rig without duplicating character logic. Wing construction uses articulated segments and controlled overlap so folding, reaching, and flapping retain the bird’s silhouette from the game camera.

Two-bird gestures are authored as one coordinated sequence with approach, contact, hold, and release phases. Each participant resolves the same partner relationship and timing from room state, while local alignment keeps the bodies from separating during contact. Short reactions use a hold-and-release radial selector; longer gestures reserve both actors until the shared sequence has completed.

05 Rest

Fall asleep together and share a lucid dream of flying

The fireside makes stillness a shared activity. Two birds can settle beside the warmth while the lounge continues around them; when each player is ready, a short breathing interaction carries them from the quiet room into flight.

Napping together

Make room for doing almost nothing

Two birds can settle on cushions beside the fire while the lounge continues around them. Their sleeping poses and breathing remain visible to anyone who walks past, leaving a clear invitation to sit down or join them.

Meditation and Dream entry

Follow three breaths into the Dream

After settling beside the fire, each player follows a short breathing rhythm. The breathing exercise builds toward entering a shared dream state, inspired by lucid dreaming and films such as Inception. The Dream is another layer conjured within the game—a game inside a game—where the birds can fly together.

How it was built

The transition prepares a much larger scene without breaking the fiction or making a sleeping bird disappear abruptly from the lounge.

Resting, the individual breathing interaction, and preparation of the Dream happen in sequence. The visible ritual uses waiting time as part of the transition instead of exposing a progress bar.

Methodology · Orchestrating the Lounge-to-Dream transition

The transition is divided into explicit states: seated rest, sleep, the private breathing sequence, Dream preparation, and entry. The lounge keeps the sleeping bird present while the destination scene loads, and the server records Dream membership and a shared world seed so both players arrive in the same landscape even when they finish breathing at different moments.

The breathing timeline coordinates the expanding ring, stars, camera treatment, and scene readiness from one progression value. Loading work happens behind that authored rhythm, allowing the visual transition and technical preparation to complete as one continuous event.

From stillness to movementThe fire does not end the evening. It becomes the boundary to the Dream, where the same wish to remain together expands into soaring through the sky together.

06 Flight

Learn to fly by feeling momentum

Entering the valley and taking flightSilent loop

Flight becomes richer as the player learns to build and release momentum. A dive gathers speed; pulling out turns that speed into height; a steep bank spends energy and can cost altitude. Space adds a discrete wingbeat rather than acting as a held throttle, so recovery comes from timing a burst and shaping the bird’s path. The controls remain forgiving near the edge without removing the consequences of a careless turn.

How it was built

The controls change pitch, roll, wing shape, and discrete flaps. A fixed-step flight model then applies lift, drag, gravity, and momentum to produce the path.

Running the simulation at a fixed rate keeps the same manoeuvre consistent across different display speeds. Recorded flights and Playwright-driven manoeuvres reproduce the same dive, turn, or pull-out while the handling is refined.

Methodology · Flight physics and control design

The flight model is a dependency-free six-degree-of-freedom simulation advanced at a fixed timestep. It derives lift from angle of attack and wing profile, combines induced and parasitic drag, applies gravity and momentum, and integrates velocity before position. A bank reduces vertical lift and curves the velocity into a coordinated turn, while pitch stability draws the body toward its flight path.

Player input controls attitude, wing shape, and discrete wingbeats. Diving converts height into speed; pulling out redirects that energy into a climb; tight turns consume energy through lift and drag. Keyboard, touch, gamepad, and scripted inputs all resolve to the same control structure, and one-tick interpolation gives the renderer a smooth pose without changing the underlying physics.

The simulation is exposed through a development harness that can place the bird, set controls, inspect aerodynamic readouts, and replay a defined manoeuvre. This makes a dive, bank, flap, or pull-out reproducible while keeping the final handling connected to the same model used in the game.

07 Exploring the Dream

Give every flight somewhere worth going

Following the river through the lower valleyFrom open sky to ground-level detail

From high above, rivers, ridges, forests, and floating islands define broad routes across the Dream. Closer to the ground, channels can be skimmed, clearings crossed, and trees flown through. These changes in scale give two flyers reasons to choose a route, circle back, or slow down for something they noticed together.

Flying together

Find, follow, and regroup

Two birds can enter the same Dream and fly through the same valley. Either player can lead toward a landmark, break away behind a ridge, or circle back to meet the other.

Floating islands

Choose an island and cross the gap

A floating island offers a gap to cross, an arch to fly beneath, a high edge to land on, or terrain you can circle around. Its outline is clipped from real terrain elevation , producing realistic coastlines and openings that can be approached from several directions.

Landscape exploration

Move from the map-scale view to the ground

The 3.8 by 8.8 kilometre range begins with real USGS elevation data. Rivers, vegetation, water, and landmarks were added to enrich the fast flight with things to pass by and scenic views to observe.

How shared exploration was built

Each client simulates its own bird. Remote birds are reconstructed from compact motion updates and eased toward new positions instead of snapping between them; deterministic generation gives both players the same terrain and islands.

The Three.js scene adds trees, shorelines, and water where they clarify a route without overwhelming the browser. Automated probes measure position error, terrain consistency, and rendering cost.

Methodology · Terrain, vegetation, and the line-art render

The Dream begins with public-domain USGS elevation data processed into a browser-ready heightfield. River channels, banks, floating landforms, and flight clearances are derived from that terrain. Seeded procedural builders then place the same landscape on every client, so navigation, collision, and shared landmarks agree across the multiplayer session.

Vegetation is composed through a six-part cover budget for canopy, shrub, herb, grass, rock, and soil. The same normalized shares drive both ground colour and planted geometry, keeping forested colour tied to visible growth. Broad habitat functions, stand-density fields, slope, wetness, and terrain folds create mixed edges, open clearings, riparian corridors, and species variation across altitude.

The line-art pass depends on readable form before surface detail. Terrain was pre-checked with the same edge operator used by the renderer, tree crowns were built as closed silhouettes, and species vary principally through proportions that remain distinct at flight distance. Tiled geometry, level of detail, frustum culling, and concentrated pockets of ground detail preserve the scale of the range within a browser rendering budget.

Methodology · Multiplayer state and shared flight

A Colyseus room owns the facts every player must share: presence, seats, media, remote ownership, activities, and Dream membership. Typed schemas replicate this state into one client-side snapshot, while private controls such as the breathing interaction and camera mode remain local. Invite codes live in the URL, allowing another player to enter the same persistent room directly.

Each player’s browser simulates its own bird. Shared flight transmits a compact motion and pose packet; the receiving client reconstructs the remote flight state, applies the same wing-pose function used by the local bird, and advances between updates with capped dead reckoning and correction. Both clients generate the landscape from the same seed, so remote motion is presented against identical terrain and landmarks.

From the experience to the methodBuilding activities that depend on camera angle, animation, terrain, and atmosphere exposed another problem: written feedback alone could not reliably point an AI collaborator at what looked wrong.

Collaborating with AI

How do you point an AI at a problem in 3D space?

Language models have gotten exceptionally good at following written instructions and correcting defects in code. But a visual defect may exist only from one camera angle: In an in-game landscape , a texture can break along a cliff, or walkable ground doesn't render correctly. I built an annotation tool inside the Three.js character and landscape studios. It pauses the scene, preserves the camera and spatial context, and lets me orbit, zoom, draw over the defect, and attach a sentence. This was my approach to multimodal visual grounding: giving an iterative coding agent a marked image, a reproducible viewpoint, and a concrete place in the 3D world to investigate.

How spatial annotation works

The saved note keeps the image, camera pose, scene context, marked region, and written comment together. The agent can use that packet to narrow the relevant object or terrain area, make a correction, and reopen the same framing.

The tool reduced the effort spent explaining where a defect lived and made persistent problems easier to track across attempts.

How visual comparisons were made repeatable

Each revision is recaptured from the same camera position as the marked image, with the earlier view retained beside it. The comparison shows exactly what changed in the model, texture, pose, or terrain.

Playwright reopens routes, repeats interactions, and captures consistent frames. This joins repeatable technical checks with direct visual comparison of appearance and motion.

Methodology · Specialist studios and reproducible harnesses

Development-only studio routes isolate one domain at a time: the procedural bird on a turntable, an interaction between a character and prop, the room layout, a landscape specimen, or the flight rig. Each studio renders the game’s actual Three.js components and pose functions. Query parameters select the subject, state, camera, and comparison mode, while an in-scene marker reports when the requested frame is ready.

Playwright capture drivers open those routes and produce repeatable stills, timed frame strips, orbit views, and side-by-side comparisons. For animation, a baked clock holds multiple rigs at the same phase; for interaction work, named cameras show the action axis and contact points; for landscape work, one asset can be staged on real terrain beside its visual reference. Adjustable controls expose the parameters that belong to that subsystem while leaving unrelated systems fixed.

The harnesses turn a visual observation into a controlled reconstruction. The same state, pose, viewpoint, and timing can be reopened after a change, making the result visible in the subsystem where it was produced and in the components used by the game.

Methodology · Spatial annotation and AI collaboration

When a mark is drawn over the paused scene, the tool raycasts through the area enclosed by the annotation and records which scene objects those rays intersect. Hits are grouped by geometry-derived subject IDs; the strongest match identifies the likely target, while other matches remain attached as ambiguity information. The work item also stores world and local coordinates, camera position, camera target, scene state, and the written description.

An AI collaborator receives a concrete packet: the annotated frame, the named object or terrain region, spatial coordinates, the viewing conditions that reveal the issue, and a command that reopens the same studio configuration. This narrows investigation to the relevant geometry, material, animation channel, or terrain function and gives each attempted correction the same viewpoint for comparison.

The workflow joins language with observable spatial context. A written request explains the intended result; the marked image establishes where the discrepancy appears; the stored scene state makes it reproducible; and the recapture shows whether the visual result changed at that exact location.

Where it goes next

The next chapter belongs to the people who gather here

Testing Birb Hangout with a few members of my family has already been a wonderful and encouraging experience. The next thing I look forward to is sharing the game with more people who could also benefit from this shared space, and find different reasons to cherish the game and return to it for another day.

What I hope to learn from future gatherings

I want to see what activities people resonate with the most, and in what ways can I make the experience easy to access and frictionless for the people.

The project matters most in the unprompted choices: where people linger, what they return to, and how they make the space their own.
